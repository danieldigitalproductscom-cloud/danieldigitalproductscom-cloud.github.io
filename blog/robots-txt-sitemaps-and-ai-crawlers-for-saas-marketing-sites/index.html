Robots.txt, Sitemaps, and AI Crawlers: A Technical SEO Setup Guide for AI SaaS Sites

Most founders touch robots.txt once, copy a snippet from a five-year-old Stack Overflow answer, and never look at it again. That's usually fine — until a page you wanted indexed quietly disappears from search, or an AI crawler you never noticed is scraping your entire pricing page for a competitor's training run. The rules here are simple once you separate what each file actually controls, and 2026 has added a genuinely new decision on top: what to do about AI crawlers.

1. Robots.txt controls crawling, not indexing

This is the single most common misunderstanding in technical SEO. A Disallow rule in robots.txt tells a well-behaved crawler "don't fetch this URL" — it does not tell search engines "don't show this URL in results." If another site links to a page you've disallowed, Google can still index the URL using just the link text and surrounding context, producing a bare, unhelpful search result with no description. If you actually want a page kept out of search results, use a <meta name="robots" content="noindex"> tag on the page itself (which requires the page to be crawlable, not disallowed) or an X-Robots-Tag HTTP header. Disallow and noindex solve different problems; using the wrong one is why "blocked" pages keep showing up in search anyway.

2. What actually belongs in robots.txt for a marketing site

Keep it short. A typical AI SaaS marketing site needs to block internal search-result pages, staging/preview paths, admin routes, and duplicate parameterized URLs (tracking params, sort/filter query strings) — not the pages that make you money. Do not disallow /pricing, /blog, or your product pages "to be safe." A minimal, correct file looks like:

User-agent: *
Disallow: /admin/
Disallow: /*?utm_
Disallow: /search
Sitemap: https://yourdomain.com/sitemap.xml

That last line matters more than people expect — pointing directly to your sitemap from robots.txt is one of the few places every major crawler, human and AI alike, reliably checks first.

3. Crawl budget almost never matters for you — yet

Crawl budget (how many pages a search engine bothers to fetch from your site in a given period) is a real constraint for sites with millions of URLs — large e-commerce catalogs, forums, marketplaces. A marketing site with a homepage, a pricing page, a dozen blog posts, and a handful of landing page variants is nowhere near that threshold; Google will happily crawl every page you have multiple times a week. Don't spend time "optimizing crawl budget" at this stage — spend it making sure the pages that exist are worth crawling. The one exception: if you programmatically generate hundreds of near-duplicate landing page variants (city pages, integration pages, industry pages), that's when crawl budget and duplicate-content management start to matter, and thin, unindexed variants should carry a noindex until they have genuinely distinct content.

4. Deciding what to do about AI crawlers

Since 2024, a new category of bot has shown up in server logs: GPTBot and OAI-SearchBot (OpenAI), ClaudeBot and Claude-SearchBot (Anthropic), PerplexityBot, Google-Extended (which controls Gemini and AI Overviews' use of your content, separate from classic Googlebot), and CCBot (Common Crawl, which feeds many models indirectly). Each respects its own user-agent line in robots.txt, and each has a different tradeoff:

  • Allowing them gives you a shot at being cited or summarized when someone asks an AI assistant a question your content answers — increasingly a real referral source, and the entire premise behind writing for generative engine optimization in the first place.
  • Blocking them keeps your specific wording out of model training runs and third-party AI answers you don't control, at the cost of that visibility.

For a pre-revenue AI SaaS site trying to get found, the default that makes sense is: allow the search-and-answer bots (OAI-SearchBot, Claude-SearchBot, PerplexityBot, Google-Extended) since being cited by an AI assistant is a distribution channel, and consider blocking pure-training crawlers like CCBot on any page you'd rather keep proprietary (pricing calculators, internal tools). You can set this per-bot:

User-agent: OAI-SearchBot
Allow: /

User-agent: CCBot
Disallow: /

There's no universally correct answer here — it's a real business tradeoff, not a technical default, so make the call deliberately rather than by leaving whatever template you copied in place.

5. Consider adding an llms.txt file

llms.txt is an emerging, not-yet-standardized convention — a plain-Markdown file at your site root that gives AI systems a curated summary of what your site is and links to the pages worth reading, similar in spirit to a sitemap but written for language models instead of crawlers. Support is inconsistent and it's not a guaranteed ranking or citation signal yet, but it costs almost nothing to add: a short description of your product, your audience, and links to your three or four most important pages. Low effort, plausible upside, worth doing once your core SEO is already solid.

6. Sitemap hygiene: the part people skip

A sitemap.xml that lists dead pages, noindexed pages, or redirect chains actively wastes crawler trust. Keep it to canonical, indexable, 200-status URLs only, update lastmod when a page's content genuinely changes (not on every deploy), and resubmit it in Google Search Console and Bing Webmaster Tools after a major restructuring — don't assume they'll notice on their own schedule. Priority and changefreq attributes are safe to omit entirely; Google has said for years it ignores both.

The checklist

  • robots.txt blocks admin/search/tracking-param paths only — never your money pages
  • Sitemap.xml is linked from robots.txt and contains only canonical, indexable URLs
  • Pages you want out of search use noindex, not Disallow
  • You've made a deliberate, not default, call on each major AI crawler
  • An llms.txt exists once the basics above are solid

None of this requires a developer sprint — most of it is a single text file you can write and deploy in an afternoon. If you're setting up a new product page from a template, get this in place on day one; it's far easier to start correct than to untangle a year of accidental disallows later.
