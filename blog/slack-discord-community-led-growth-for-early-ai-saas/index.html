Community-Led Growth: Should Your Early AI SaaS Have a Slack or Discord?

Somewhere around the first hundred customers, most AI SaaS founders ask the same question: should we start a Slack or Discord? The honest answer is that a community is not a growth channel you turn on — it's a second product you have to run, with its own onboarding, its own moderation, and its own failure mode: a room with twelve people in it, nobody posting, and a join link on your landing page quietly advertising that fact to every new visitor. Done well, though, a small active community becomes your cheapest support desk, your best source of feature-request signal, and a referral engine that runs without you. Here's how to decide, and how to set it up so it doesn't become the ghost town.

1. Don't start one until you have a reason people would show up daily

A community needs a recurring reason to open it, not just a reason to join it. "Get support" is a reason to join, not a reason to come back — people show up once, ask their question, and leave the tab muted forever. "Compare notes with other people doing the same job" is a reason to return. If your product serves a niche where practitioners already talk shop — indie hackers, RevOps leads, prompt engineers — you have that ingredient. If your product is a general consumer tool with no shared professional identity among users, a community usually stays quiet no matter how well you run it, and a changelog or newsletter will outperform it for the same effort.

2. Slack for professionals who are already in Slack all day; Discord for everyone else

The channel choice is mostly about where your buyer already has muscle memory. B2B and professional audiences (ops, marketing, engineering leads) tolerate Slack because they're already logged in for work; asking them to also open Discord is friction they'll skip. Consumer, creator, and technical-hobbyist audiences are more often already on Discord and treat it as a hangout, not a work tool — the more casual, always-on culture fits community better than it fits Slack's channel-per-topic formality. Don't run both from day one; pick the one your users are already fluent in and go deep there.

3. Seed it with real conversation before you announce it publicly

An empty community that gets announced to your whole list dies in a week — everyone joins, sees silence, and never returns. Invite twenty to thirty of your most engaged existing users personally, seed a handful of genuinely useful threads yourself (a real tip, a real question, a "here's what we're building next and why"), and let it develop a baseline pulse before you put the join link on your landing page. The link should arrive once there's something to walk into, not before.

4. Give it a job your support inbox doesn't do well

The strongest reason a community outperforms a support inbox is peer-to-peer answers: another user explaining a workaround, sharing a template, or vouching for you when someone asks "is this worth paying for" in public. Structure a few channels explicitly around that — a channel for sharing what people built, a channel for asking other users (not just you) how they solved something, a channel for early access to new features in exchange for feedback. If every channel just routes back to your support team, you've rebuilt your inbox with worse search.

5. Show up as a founder, not as a support bot

The single biggest predictor of whether an early community stays alive is whether the founder is visibly present — answering questions personally, reacting, occasionally posting something unfinished and asking what people think. That presence is what makes an early member feel like they're talking to the company, not filing a ticket. It doesn't scale forever, but in the first few hundred members it's the entire reason people stay engaged instead of treating the space as a black hole.

6. Moderate lightly, but moderate

Most early communities don't die from bad actors; they die from low-effort self-promotion drowning out real conversation once word gets out that people show up there. A short, visible set of rules ("no cold-pitching your own product outside the designated channel") and a founder who enforces them consistently keeps the signal-to-noise ratio high enough that engaged users keep reading. Waiting until it's a problem to write the rules is too late — the users who would have kept posting have already muted the channel.

7. Measure it like a channel, not a vanity metric

Member count is close to meaningless on its own — a community with 3,000 silent members is worse for your business than one with 150 active ones. Track weekly active posters, the number of support questions answered by other members instead of your team, and how many new signups mention the community as how they heard about you. If none of those numbers move for a couple of months after a genuine seeding effort, that's a real signal to fold the effort back into support and content instead of continuing to staff a room nobody uses.

The honest tradeoff

A community is a compounding asset when it works — new users get faster answers from peers instead of a queue, and some of your best feature ideas and testimonials will come from threads you didn't start. But it's also one of the few growth channels that visibly broadcasts failure: a dead Slack link on a landing page tells a skeptical visitor more about your traction than an honest "we're small" ever would. If you're not ready to seed it, show up in it daily, and moderate it for the first few months, it's better to skip the link entirely than to publish evidence of a room nobody's in.
