Public Roadmap Pages: The Support-Deflection and SEO Asset Most AI SaaS Founders Skip

Every founder answers the same question a dozen times a week, in a dozen different channels: "does it do X yet, and if not, when?" It shows up in support tickets, sales calls, Twitter replies, and Slack DMs from trial users who are one missing feature away from either upgrading or churning. A public roadmap page answers that question once, permanently, in a place Google can index and a support agent can link to instead of retyping the same paragraph. Almost no early-stage AI/SaaS product has one, which is exactly why it's worth the afternoon it takes to build.

The three-column structure that actually works

Skip elaborate kanban boards. A roadmap page needs exactly three columns, and the labels matter more than they seem: Shipped, In Progress, and Planned — not "Q3" or "Q4," because AI/SaaS roadmaps slip constantly and a quarter label you miss becomes a trust problem, while "In Progress" is true until it isn't. Each item gets a one-line description written in the user's language ("Bulk export to CSV," not "Implement streaming export pipeline"), and Shipped items link straight to the changelog entry or release note that explains them. That link is what turns a static list into a page people actually return to.

What belongs on it — and what quietly damages trust

Put feature requests on it that you've genuinely committed engineering time to, not everything anyone has ever asked for. A roadmap crowded with fifty aspirational bullet points reads as either dishonest or directionless, and trial users notice the difference between a curated list and a wish-dumping ground. Leave off anything tied to a specific date you're not confident in — "coming soon" ages fine; "shipping March 2026" sitting untouched in August does not. Also leave off anything that reveals competitive strategy in detail (pricing model changes, enterprise-only gating plans); a public roadmap is a support and trust tool, not an internal planning doc, and competitors read these pages too.

Let people vote, but moderate the input

A simple upvote count next to each planned item does two things: it gives you a real, quantified signal for prioritization instead of "the loudest customer in Slack," and it gives requesters a feeling of being heard without you promising anything. The trap is treating vote count as a binding contract — a feature with ninety votes from free-tier users can matter less than one request from a $2,000/mo account, and your roadmap copy should never imply votes alone decide the order. If you don't have engineering bandwidth to build a voting widget, a plain "email us to support this" link under each item works fine at small scale; the point is capturing the signal somewhere other than scattered DMs.

The support-deflection math is the real ROI

Track how often your support team currently answers "do you support X" or "is Y on the roadmap" — even a rough week-long tally in your help desk tags is enough. Every one of those tickets that a linked roadmap page deflects is time back for a founder or a support hire, and unlike a canned macro reply, a public roadmap page lets the user self-serve the answer before they even open a ticket. Link it from three places: your help center's top-level nav, your pricing page FAQ ("is feature X planned?"), and your in-app upgrade or feature-gate prompts, since that's exactly the moment a user is asking the question.

It's an SEO asset too, almost by accident

Roadmap pages naturally accumulate the exact long-tail phrases prospects search before buying: "does [product] support SSO," "[product] Salesforce integration," "[product] roadmap." Because the page updates over time and links out to changelog entries, search engines treat it as a living, frequently-refreshed page rather than static marketing copy — a pattern that tends to earn more frequent recrawls than a page that never changes. Write each roadmap item as a real sentence a searcher might type, not an internal ticket title, and you'll pick up impressions for exactly the comparison searches happening right before a purchase decision.

Keep it honest by pruning aggressively

The single fastest way to turn a roadmap page from an asset into a liability is letting "In Progress" items sit there for six months with no movement. Review the page monthly: anything stalled moves back to "Planned" with an honest note, anything shipped moves out within a week of release, and anything you've quietly decided not to build gets removed rather than left to rot — a dead promise is worse than no promise. A roadmap page only earns trust as long as it's clearly still being tended; the moment it looks abandoned, it reads as a broken promise instead of a plan.

If you're building the surrounding activation and onboarding experience at the same time, a roadmap page slots naturally next to your changelog and help center — the same trio that turns a trial user's "will this actually keep improving" hesitation into confidence before the free trial runs out.
