Unlinked Mentions and Broken Links: The Fastest Backlinks an AI SaaS Startup Can Reclaim

Most link-building advice starts with asking strangers for links. Link reclamation starts somewhere easier: places that already know your product exists. Someone wrote about you without linking, linked to a URL you later deleted, or used an old product name. Fixing these costs far less effort than cold outreach and the response rate is much higher, because the writer has already decided you are worth mentioning.

The three kinds of reclaimable links

  • Unlinked brand mentions. An article, newsletter, or directory names your product or founder but gives no hyperlink.
  • Broken inbound links. A page links to your site, but the target URL now returns a 404 or a redirect chain, so little or none of the value reaches you.
  • Outdated references. A page links correctly, but uses a former product name, old pricing, or a retired feature, or points to a redirected domain.

Step 1: Find unlinked mentions

You do not need a paid suite to start. Combine a few free methods:

  • Search operators. Search for "YourProduct" -site:yourdomain.com. Add -inurl:twitter -inurl:linkedin to cut noise. Run variants for your founder name and common misspellings.
  • Time filters. Use the search tools menu to restrict results to the past month so you can run the same search monthly and only see new mentions.
  • Alerts. Set up a free alert for your exact product name so new mentions arrive in your inbox.
  • Product-name collisions. If your name is a common word, add context terms such as "AI", "SaaS", or your category, or you will drown in irrelevant results.

Open each result and check whether the page links to you. Many mentions turn out to be linked already; only keep the ones that are not.

Step 2: Qualify before you write

Not every mention deserves an email. Score each one quickly:

  • Relevance. Is the page about your topic or audience? A link from a relevant article beats ten from unrelated directories.
  • Editable by a person. Blog posts and newsletters archives can be updated. Forum threads, social posts, and some news items usually cannot.
  • Context. Is the mention positive or neutral? Do not ask for a link on a page that criticizes you; that is a support conversation, not a link request.
  • Findable contact. A named author with a visible contact method is better than a generic inbox.

Ten qualified targets will be worth more than a hundred poor ones.

Step 3: Write the request

Keep it short, specific, and easy to say yes to. A workable structure:

  1. Thank them for the piece, naming it specifically so it is obvious you read it.
  2. Point out the exact sentence where your product is mentioned.
  3. Offer the exact URL to link to and explain, in one line, why it helps readers (for example, it goes to the docs page that shows the feature they described).
  4. Make no demands and offer to help with anything that needs correcting.

Send one message, and at most one polite follow-up a week or two later. Do not send bulk, templated messages with merge fields you have not checked; they read as spam and risk your sender reputation. Never offer payment for links, which breaks search engine guidelines and can get a site penalized.

Step 4: Reclaim broken inbound links

This one is mostly technical and you control it fully.

  • Find them. In Google Search Console, open the Links report and review top linked pages. Cross-check those URLs against your site: any that return 404 are lost value. A backlink tool can list 404 targets directly if you have one.
  • Redirect, do not recreate blindly. If the old page had a close equivalent, add a single 301 redirect to it. If there is no equivalent, consider restoring the page, since a live page with real links is an asset.
  • Avoid chains. Point the old URL straight at the final destination rather than through two or three hops.
  • Do not redirect everything to the homepage. Search engines may treat irrelevant redirects as soft 404s, and visitors who land on the wrong page leave.
  • Fix typos in your own redirects. A common source of broken links is a link someone copied with a trailing character or wrong case. A rule that handles the common variation recovers it without contacting anyone.

Before any migration, rename, or URL cleanup, export your top linked URLs first. Many startups lose their best links during a redesign and only notice months later when rankings slip.

Step 5: Update outdated references

If you renamed the product or moved domains, search for the old name using the same operator method. Ask the author to update the name and link, framing it as keeping their article accurate. Also keep a redirect from the old domain in place for as long as possible, since you will keep receiving links that were written before the change.

Make mentions linkable in the first place

Prevent future losses by making it easy for writers to link correctly:

  • A stable, short URL for the product and a press or brand page with the correct name, one-line description, and logo files.
  • Consistent naming. Use one spelling and capitalization everywhere so mentions are searchable.
  • Linkable assets. Pages worth linking to, such as docs, a changelog, a pricing page, or original research, give writers something specific to point at instead of a bare name.

A monthly routine that takes about two hours

  1. Run your saved mention searches filtered to the last month and log new results in a spreadsheet.
  2. Check each for an existing link and qualify the rest.
  3. Send up to ten personalized requests and note the date.
  4. Review Search Console for new 404 targets with inbound links and add redirects.
  5. Record which requests led to links so you can see your real success rate.

Common mistakes

  • Asking for a link when the page already has one, which wastes goodwill. Always check first.
  • Pushing for exact-match anchor text. Let the writer link naturally; a branded or descriptive anchor is fine and safer.
  • Redirecting every dead URL to the homepage. Match each to the most relevant live page or leave a real 404 or 410 when there is no equivalent.
  • Ignoring tracking. Without a record, you cannot tell whether the effort pays off.
  • Treating it as a one-off. Mentions accumulate as you grow, so a short recurring routine beats a big annual push.

Reclamation will not replace creating things worth linking to, but it is the lowest-cost link work available because the hard part, being noticed, has already happened. Make a list of your current mentions, fix your broken inbound URLs this week, and repeat the process monthly.
