Keyword Research for AI/SaaS Founders Who Don’t Have an SEO Budget

Most early-stage keyword research goes wrong before it starts: a founder opens a paid tool, exports five hundred keywords sorted by search volume, and ends up with a spreadsheet of terms that are either too competitive to ever rank for or too generic to convert if they did. You don't need a paid tool to do this well. You need a process that finds the terms your actual future customers type, sized honestly, mapped to the right page. Here's the version that works with zero budget.

1. Start from questions, not head terms

Don't start by guessing at "AI scheduling tool" and checking its volume — that term is dominated by category leaders and tells you nothing about intent. Start from the actual questions your buyer types when they hit the problem: "how to stop double-booking meetings across time zones," "why does my calendar app not sync with Slack." Google's autocomplete and the "People also ask" box on a results page are a free, real-time list of exactly these questions, sourced from real searches, updated constantly. Type your core problem in incognito mode, screenshot every autocomplete suggestion and every "People also ask" expansion, and you'll have thirty to sixty real phrases in under an hour.

2. Read the results page before you write anything

For every candidate keyword, search it and actually look at what's ranking. This single step replaces most of what a paid tool tries to estimate. If the top ten results are all enterprise vendors with G2 badges and demo-request forms, that's a buyer-intent term dominated by funded competitors — hard to win as a new page, but a signal the topic converts, so it's worth a comparison or alternative-to page later. If the top results are forum threads, Reddit posts, or thin blog posts with no real authority behind them, that's a gap: the demand exists and the supply is weak. Those are the terms worth writing for first.

3. Size demand with what's actually free

Google Trends won't give you a search-volume number, but it will tell you, honestly, whether a topic is flat, rising, or dying, and it's free with no account. Google Search Console (once your site has any traffic at all) shows you the exact queries people already use to find your existing pages, including ones you never targeted on purpose — that's real, first-party demand data, not an estimate. Google's own Keyword Planner still returns a bucketed volume range if you create a free Ads account and never spend a dollar; the ranges are wide, but "10–100 searches/month" versus "1K–10K searches/month" is enough resolution to prioritize a list without paying for precision you don't need yet.

4. Steal your competitors' rankings, not their copy

Search site:competitor.com combined with a topic word (for example site:competitor.com pricing or site:competitor.com vs) to see which pages they've built around a theme, then open each one and note the exact phrasing in its title tag and headers — that phrasing is a strong hint at the keyword they targeted, since most SEO-aware teams keep the two aligned. You're not copying the page; you're reading it as a keyword list someone else already validated with their own research.

5. Bucket every keyword by the page it belongs on

A keyword without a destination page is just a note to yourself. Sort every term you collect into one of four buckets before you write anything: homepage/product terms ("[category] for [audience]") that belong on your landing page's title and hero copy; comparison terms ("[you] vs [competitor]", "[competitor] alternative") that need their own dedicated page, not a paragraph buried in a features page; how-to and educational terms that belong in a blog post aimed at people who aren't ready to buy yet but will be; and bottom-funnel terms ("best [category] for [use case]", "[category] pricing") that deserve a page built to convert, not just inform. Writing a how-to post for a bottom-funnel keyword — or trying to rank your homepage for a long-tail question — is the single most common way founders waste the research they just did.

6. Watch for cannibalization before it happens

Once you have more than a handful of pages, it's easy to accidentally target the same keyword twice — a blog post and a landing page both quietly competing for "AI meeting notes app," splitting your own authority between them. Before publishing a new page, search the exact keyword site-restricted to your own domain (site:yourdomain.com "AI meeting notes app") to check whether you already have a page ranking or trying to rank for it. If you do, expand that page instead of creating a competitor to yourself.

7. Re-run the process from Search Console, not from scratch

Once your site has a few months of traffic, the best keyword research tool you have is free and already installed: Search Console's Performance report, filtered to queries where you rank between positions 5 and 20. Those are terms Google already thinks you're relevant for — you just need a stronger page, a better title tag, or more depth to move up. Chasing "position 8 to position 3" on a term with existing traction is almost always higher-leverage than chasing a brand-new keyword at position zero, and it costs nothing to find.

The pattern underneath all of this

Paid keyword tools sell precision on search volume, but precision on volume was never the hard part — matching real intent to the right page is. Autocomplete, "People also ask," Trends, a free Ads account, and your own Search Console data get you close enough to the real numbers, and the discipline of bucketing every term by destination page is what actually turns a keyword list into traffic. Do the free version well before you pay for a tool to do it faster.
