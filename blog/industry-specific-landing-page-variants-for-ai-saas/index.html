Industry-Specific Landing Pages: Ranking for Vertical Searches Without Building Doorway Pages

Once an AI SaaS product has real traction, the search terms that show up in Search Console start splitting by industry: "fraud detection for marketplaces," "fraud detection for insurance," "fraud detection for payments." The instinct is to spin up a page per vertical fast — duplicate the homepage, swap the industry noun, ship it. That's also the exact pattern Google's own guidance describes as a doorway page, and search engines have gotten good at collapsing near-identical variants into a single indexed result, which means most of that effort ranks for nothing. Here's the bar a vertical page actually has to clear to be worth building.

1. The test: could a reader tell which industry this page is for with the product name removed?

Swap "insurance" for "payments" in a page and read it again. If every sentence still makes sense unchanged, you built a doorway page, not a vertical page. A real variant names the specific fraud pattern that industry deals with ("policy-stacking and staged-claim fraud" for insurance vs. "card-testing and account-takeover" for payments), the specific integration that matters to that buyer (a claims system vs. a payment gateway), and the specific compliance language that industry's buyers actually ask about. If you can't fill in three or four industry-specific facts like that, you don't have enough material for a dedicated page yet — fold that vertical into a features list on the main page instead.

2. Only build a variant where you have real proof, not just a keyword

Keyword research will hand you a dozen plausible verticals. Real differentiated proof — a customer in that industry, a stat specific to that use case, a compliance certification that industry cares about — will hand you far fewer. Build the page for the second list, not the first. A vertical page backed by a genuine case study or a named integration reads as expertise; the same page with a generic feature list and a changed headline reads as thin no matter how well-written the prose is, and both search engines and buyers are increasingly good at telling the difference.

3. Give each variant its own indexable URL and its own title tag

A tab-switcher or a dropdown that swaps industry copy via JavaScript on a single URL can't rank for "[product] for insurance" as a distinct result — search engines index the URL, not the state a visitor happened to select. Each vertical needs its own path (/industries/insurance, /industries/payments), its own <title> and meta description naming that vertical, and its own H1. This is the same lesson as an integrations page done right: an index page ranks for nothing specific, individual pages each rank for one thing precisely.

4. Build a hub, don't leave variants orphaned

An "Industries" index page linking to every vertical, and every vertical page linking back to the index and to one or two adjacent verticals, does two things: it helps a visitor who landed on the wrong vertical find the right one in one click, and it gives search engines a clear internal-linking signal that these are a deliberate, related set of pages rather than scattered, disconnected content. A vertical page with zero internal links pointing to it is nearly invisible no matter how well it's written — internal links are often the only path a crawler has to find it.

5. Vary the proof, not just the noun

The parts of the page that should actually change between verticals: the named fraud pattern, the specific integration or data source, any industry-specific regulation (PCI DSS for payments, HIPAA-adjacent claims data for insurance-adjacent healthcare products), and a testimonial or metric from that vertical if you have one. The parts that can stay constant: your core methodology, your pricing structure, your general product screenshots. Changing everything wastes effort on cosmetic differences; changing nothing produces a doorway page. The line between them is proof — vary whatever is actually true and specific to that industry, keep the rest as your honest baseline.

6. Don't build a vertical page before you have the traffic to justify it

A common failure mode is building fifteen vertical pages in a launch sprint, most for industries with a handful of monthly searches and zero customers. Thin, unmaintained pages drag down how search engines evaluate the whole cluster, including the verticals that do have real demand. Start with the two or three verticals where you already have a customer or a clear signal of search volume, prove the pattern converts, and expand from there — the same discipline that makes a glossary or an integrations section work applies here too.

7. Route the wrong-fit visitor somewhere useful, not to a dead end

Someone who lands on your insurance page while actually working in a vertical you haven't built yet shouldn't hit a wall. A short "not quite your industry? See how it works for [general audience]" link back to the main product page keeps that visitor in the funnel instead of losing them to a page that clearly wasn't written for them. This is a small addition, but it's the difference between a vertical page that's a net addition to your funnel and one that quietly cannibalizes visitors who would have converted fine on the general page.

8. Retire pages that never earn their keep

Check Search Console every quarter for which vertical pages are actually getting impressions and clicks. A page that's sat at zero for six months isn't a future asset quietly waiting to rank — it's dead weight that dilutes your site's overall content quality signal. Either invest real, specific content into it (a genuine case study, a real integration) or fold it back into the general product page and redirect the URL. A smaller set of strong vertical pages consistently outperforms a large set of thin ones, both in rankings and in the impression it leaves on a visitor comparing your site to a competitor's.

The pattern underneath all of this

A vertical landing page earns its place in a search index the same way a comparison page or a case study does: by containing something specific and true that a generic page couldn't say. The failure mode isn't "not enough vertical pages" — it's vertical pages that are the same page wearing a different industry's name, which both readers and search engines learn to discount the moment they notice the pattern.

If you're building a product that genuinely serves buyers across different industries, start from a template with a metrics band and trust section already in place, so each vertical page you add is filling in real, industry-specific proof rather than designing layout under deadline pressure.
