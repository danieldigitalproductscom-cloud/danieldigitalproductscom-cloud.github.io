Webinar & Event Registration Pages: The Landing Page Type Most AI SaaS Founders Get Wrong

Most founders build a webinar registration page like a shrunken version of their homepage — logo, headline, a form — and then wonder why half of registrants never show up. A webinar page is selling something narrower and harder than a product: forty-five minutes of a stranger's calendar, at a specific time, for a specific payoff. Treat it like a scheduling and persuasion problem, not a signup form, and both registration and attendance rates move.

1. The headline sells the takeaway, not the topic

"Webinar: Introduction to AI Workflow Automation" tells a visitor nothing they can act on. "How three ops teams cut manual ticket routing by 70% — live walkthrough" tells them exactly what they'll leave with. Name the specific, concrete thing an attendee will know or be able to do afterward that they can't do right now. If you can't state that in one sentence, the webinar itself probably needs sharpening before the page does.

2. Show the time in the visitor's own timezone, not yours

"Thursday, October 2 at 1:00 PM ET" forces every non-US visitor to do math, and a meaningful fraction will get it wrong or give up. Detect the visitor's local timezone from the browser and display the time already converted — "1:00 PM ET / 10:00 AM PT / 6:00 PM GMT" if you can't auto-detect, or a single auto-localized time if you can. This alone measurably reduces no-shows, because the single most common reason someone misses a webinar they registered for is a timezone mix-up, not a change of heart.

3. Registration form: three fields, not eight

Name, work email, and maybe company — that's it for the form on the page itself. Every additional field ("company size," "role," "how did you hear about us") is a tax on registration volume, and you can collect that information later through a one-question post-registration survey or by enriching the email address. A webinar is a lower-commitment ask than a demo request; the form should feel proportionally lighter.

4. Put "Add to Calendar" buttons directly on the confirmation

The confirmation page and email are where most webinar pages stop trying — but this is the single highest-leverage moment for attendance. Offer one-click "Add to Google Calendar," "Add to Outlook," and a downloadable .ics file, all visible without scrolling. A registrant who adds the event to their calendar in the next ten seconds is dramatically more likely to show up than one who plans to "remember." Don't make this an afterthought link at the bottom of a confirmation email.

5. Reminder cadence: three touches, not one

One confirmation email is not a reminder sequence. Send a confirmation immediately, a reminder 24 hours before with the join link already included (not "we'll send the link closer to the date," which creates a second point of failure), and a final reminder 30–60 minutes before start. Each email should restate the specific takeaway from the headline, not just "see you soon" — people forget why they registered for something two weeks ago.

6. Speaker credibility, specific not generic

"Jane Doe, Head of Product" is a title. "Jane Doe, who led the workflow-automation rollout that cut ticket volume 40% at [recognizable company]" is a reason to show up. If your speaker doesn't have an outside credential yet, lean on the specificity of what they'll demonstrate live instead — a real account, a real before/after, not a slide deck of claims.

7. Design for the no-show, not just the registrant

Most people who register for a webinar will not attend live — that's normal, not a failure of your page. Say on the registration page itself that a recording will be sent to everyone who signs up, whether they attend or not. This single sentence increases registrations, because it removes the "I might have a conflict" objection that otherwise stops someone from signing up at all.

8. Don't let the page die after the event

This is the biggest missed opportunity on most webinar pages: the URL that was driving registration traffic goes stale the day after the event, or redirects to a generic "thanks for attending" page with no content. Instead, convert it into an evergreen on-demand page — embed the recording, publish a written summary of the key points, and keep a short "watch now" form for anyone who missed the live date. Add VideoObject structured data with the recording's duration, upload date, and description so search engines can surface it as a rich result. A good webinar recap page keeps generating registrations (now for the recording) for months, off the same content you already produced once.

9. Route attendees somewhere specific afterward

The follow-up email to attendees shouldn't just say "thanks for coming" — it should link to the exact next step tied to what they just watched: a related feature's docs, a template mentioned during the demo, or a trial signup pre-filled with context ("Start free — the workflow builder you saw today is on every plan"). No-shows get a different email: the recording link, plus the same specific next step. Treat the two audiences differently; someone who watched live is further along than someone who registered and forgot.

The pattern underneath all of this

A webinar page has to do persuasion twice — once to get the registration, once (through timezone clarity, calendar friction removal, and reminders) to actually get the person in the room. Founders who only optimize the first half end up with an impressive registration count and a live attendance rate under 20%, then wonder why the webinar "didn't work." Fix the mechanics of showing up, and the same content converts far better without changing a word of the pitch.

If you're setting this up for the first time, start from a template that already separates registration, confirmation, and on-demand states as distinct pages — so the reminder emails and calendar links aren't bolted on after the fact, and the page still has a job to do once the live date has passed.
