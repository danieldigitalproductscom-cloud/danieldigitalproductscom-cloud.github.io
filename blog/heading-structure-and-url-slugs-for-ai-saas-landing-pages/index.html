Heading Structure and URL Slugs for AI SaaS Landing Pages: Small Choices That Compound

Headings and URLs are two of the cheapest on-page elements to get right and two of the most commonly wasted. Many AI SaaS landing pages ship with an H1 that is a clever slogan, a dozen H2s that say "Features" or "Why us", and URLs like /p/7f3a9 or /solutions/ai-powered-intelligent-automation-platform-v2. Search engines and the AI assistants that summarize pages both lean on these elements to understand what a page is about. Visitors use them to scan. Here is how to set them up deliberately.

1. Write one H1 that names the thing and the outcome

Use exactly one H1 per page, and make it say what the page is about in plain words. A tagline can sit in a subheading or hero paragraph. Compare:

  • Weak: "Work, reimagined." It tells neither a crawler nor a first-time visitor what you sell.
  • Better: "AI code review that catches bugs before your team opens the pull request." It names the category (AI code review), the user (engineering teams) and the outcome.

You do not need to cram in the exact keyword phrase, but the words people would type should appear naturally. Keep the H1 close to the page's title tag in meaning without copying it verbatim: the title tag is written for the search result, the H1 for someone who has already arrived.

2. Build H2s from real questions and objections

Each H2 should label a section a reader could jump to. Generic labels waste space. Specific ones match how people search and how AI answer engines pick passages to quote. Examples for an AI support product:

  • "How the AI drafts a reply from your help center" instead of "How it works"
  • "What happens when the AI is not sure" instead of "Safety"
  • "Pricing per resolved conversation" instead of "Pricing"
  • "Integrations with Zendesk, Intercom and Slack" instead of "Integrations"

Source the phrasing from sales calls, support tickets and the questions in Search Console's query report. If a heading could appear unchanged on a competitor's page, make it more specific.

3. Respect the hierarchy

H3s belong under H2s, and you should not skip levels just to get a smaller font. Use CSS for size and the heading level for meaning. Common structural errors on SaaS sites:

  • Headings used as styling. A big stat like "98% accuracy" wrapped in an H2 pollutes the outline. Use a paragraph or a styled element instead.
  • Logo and nav text as headings. The footer's "Product", "Company" and "Legal" columns are usually not headings worth exposing at the top level of the outline.
  • Multiple H1s from components. Reusable hero blocks often hardcode an H1. Audit pages that combine several blocks.
  • Hidden headings full of keywords. Text hidden only for search engines is a policy risk and gives no value to users.

To check, open the browser's accessibility tree or use a headings outline extension and read just the headings top to bottom. If they tell the story of the page, the structure is sound. This also benefits screen reader users, who commonly navigate by heading.

4. Keep headings readable, not stuffed

Aim for headings that read naturally at about 5 to 12 words. Repeating the same keyword in every H2 looks spammy and reads poorly. If your product category is "AI meeting notes", the H2s can talk about transcripts, action items, privacy and integrations without repeating the full phrase each time. Front-load the key term where it fits, because headings are often truncated in mobile outlines and in featured snippets.

5. Choose URL slugs that are short, readable and stable

A good URL tells a human where they are. Practical rules:

  • Lowercase words separated by hyphens. Avoid underscores, spaces, capital letters and encoded characters.
  • Describe the page, not the campaign. /pricing, /integrations/slack and /use-cases/customer-support beat /q4-launch-v3.
  • Drop filler words and dates unless the date is the point. A year in a slug makes the page look stale next January and tempts you to change the URL.
  • Keep it shallow. Two or three path segments are enough. Mirror the real site hierarchy so breadcrumbs and URLs agree.
  • Avoid parameters for content. Use /templates/sales-email rather than /templates?id=44&view=full.

6. Treat a URL change as a migration

Once a page has links, shares and rankings, changing its slug throws away part of that value unless you handle it. If you must rename, set a single permanent 301 redirect from the old URL to the new one, update internal links so they point directly at the new URL, change the canonical tag and sitemap entry, and verify in Search Console. Do not chain redirects. For brand-new pages, take ten minutes to pick the slug properly so you never need this step. Decide on trailing slash and www or non-www conventions once and enforce them site-wide so the same page is not reachable at multiple addresses.

7. Use anchor-friendly heading IDs

Give long pages stable IDs on H2s (for example id="pricing") so you can link directly to sections from docs, emails and support replies, and so a table of contents works. Search results sometimes show jump links to these sections. Keep the IDs fixed when you edit heading text, otherwise old links silently break.

Quick checklist

  • One H1 that names the product category and outcome in plain language.
  • H2s that are specific, question-based and unique to your product.
  • No skipped levels, no headings used only for styling, no hidden text.
  • Headings read naturally; no repeated keyword stuffing.
  • Slugs are lowercase, hyphenated, short and free of dates or campaign names.
  • Renames use one 301, plus updated internal links, canonicals and sitemap.
  • Stable IDs on major sections for deep linking.

Reviewing a page's outline takes only a few minutes and often reveals why it fails to match what people search for.
