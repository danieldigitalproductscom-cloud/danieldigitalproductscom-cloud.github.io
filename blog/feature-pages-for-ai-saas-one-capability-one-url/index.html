Feature Pages for AI SaaS: One Capability, One URL, One Search Intent

Most AI SaaS sites describe every capability on the homepage and then wonder why they rank for nothing but their own name. A searcher typing "AI meeting action item extractor" wants a page about exactly that, not a general product overview. Feature pages give each capability its own URL, its own query, and its own path to signup. This guide covers how to choose them, structure them, and keep them from turning into thin, repetitive filler.

Decide which capabilities deserve a page

Not every feature needs a URL. A capability earns one when it passes three tests:

  • People search for it by name. Check Search Console, autocomplete, and "People also ask" for phrasing like "summarize support tickets with AI". If nobody searches for the concept, a section on a broader page is enough.
  • It is a reason people buy. Look at sales calls and onboarding surveys. Pick the two or three capabilities customers mention first, not the ones your roadmap is proudest of.
  • You can show it working. If you cannot produce a real input and output example, the page will read as marketing copy and rank accordingly.

A young product usually has three to eight qualifying pages. Start with the top three and expand only after they get impressions.

Match the page to the intent behind the query

Search the target phrase and read the first page of results. If they are tool listicles, a feature page may be the wrong format for that query. If they are product pages for individual capabilities, you are in the right lane. Typical intents you can serve with a feature page:

  • Task intent: "extract invoice line items automatically". The visitor wants the job done. Lead with the outcome and a demo.
  • Evaluation intent: "AI redlining tool for contracts". The visitor is comparing options. Lead with how it works, accuracy notes, and limits.
  • Integration intent: "summarize Zoom calls into Notion". The visitor needs confirmation it fits their stack. Lead with the connection and setup steps.

Write the page for one of these. Pages that try to serve all three tend to satisfy none.

A structure that works

  1. H1 that names the capability and the outcome. "Extract action items from meetings automatically" beats "Smart Insights". Use the words customers use, not internal product names.
  2. A one-sentence answer in the first screen. What it does, for whom, and the result. Follow with one primary call to action.
  3. A real input and output example. Show a sample transcript and the extracted tasks, or a messy PDF and the structured fields. Use text, not only a screenshot, so it can be read by crawlers and assistive tools.
  4. How it works in three to five steps. Plain description of what happens, including where a human can review or correct output.
  5. Limits and accuracy. State what it handles poorly, such as handwritten scans, heavy accents, or languages you do not support. This builds trust and filters out poor-fit signups before they churn.
  6. Who uses it and a concrete result. One short quote or a figure with its source and time frame. If you have no customers yet, use a pilot result and label it honestly.
  7. Related capabilities and integrations. Links to two or three sibling feature pages and the relevant integration pages.
  8. FAQ and a closing call to action. Answer the real questions about pricing limits, data handling, and setup time.

On-page details that matter

  • Title tag: capability plus category, around 60 characters, such as "AI Action Item Extraction for Meetings | Echo".
  • URL: short and stable, for example /features/action-items. Avoid dates and version numbers.
  • Headings: use H2s that read like questions or tasks people search for, which also helps featured snippets and AI answer engines pull a clean passage.
  • Structured data: mark up the FAQ section only if the questions and answers are visible on the page. Do not invent ratings.
  • Images: compress, set width and height, and write alt text that describes what the output shows.

Avoid the thin-page trap

The common failure is generating ten near-identical pages that swap one noun. Search engines treat these as low value, and visitors bounce. Before publishing, ask whether the page contains something the others do not: a different example, different limits, different integrations, a different customer story. If two pages would share more than half their text, merge them into one stronger page and use anchored sections.

Wire the pages into the site

  • Link from the homepage and navigation using descriptive anchor text, not "Learn more".
  • Cross-link siblings. A transcription page should point to the summary page and the action item page when they form one workflow.
  • Link from blog posts and docs whenever you mention the capability, so authority flows to the page you want to rank.
  • Include them in your XML sitemap and confirm they are indexable with no stray noindex tag.
  • Pass context into signup. Use a parameter or separate button so you can see which capability brings in trials.

Measure and improve

After a month, check each page in Search Console for the queries it appears for, its average position, and its click-through rate. In analytics, compare signup rate by entry page. A page with impressions but low clicks needs a better title. A page with clicks but no signups needs a clearer example or a better call to action. A page with neither likely targets a query nobody uses; fold it into a broader page.

Pre-publish checklist

  • One capability, one primary query, one intent.
  • Real input and output example in text.
  • Limits stated plainly.
  • Unique content not shared with sibling pages.
  • Links to and from related pages.
  • Fast load and no layout shift above the fold.
  • One clear call to action tracked by entry page.

Feature pages are how a small AI product shows up for the specific problems buyers search for, instead of competing only on a brand name nobody knows yet. Pick your top capabilities, show real output, be honest about limits, and link everything together.
