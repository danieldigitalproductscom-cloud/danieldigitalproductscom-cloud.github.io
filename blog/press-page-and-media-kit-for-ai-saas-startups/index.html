Press Page and Media Kit for AI SaaS Startups: Make It Easy for Journalists and Newsletter Writers to Cover You

When a reporter, newsletter writer, or podcast host decides to mention your product, they usually have ten minutes and a deadline. If they cannot quickly find what you do, how to spell your name, a logo, and a screenshot, they either skip you or get details wrong. A simple press page removes that friction. It also gives you one canonical, crawlable source of facts about your company, which helps both search engines and AI answer engines describe you accurately.

Why a press page is worth an afternoon

  • Better links. Writers who can grab an official logo and a one-line description are more likely to link to your site, and link to the right page.
  • Fewer factual errors. Mistakes about pricing, founding date, or what your model does tend to get copied from article to article. A canonical page gives people something to check against.
  • Brand search support. A page that names your company, founders, and category in plain text reinforces the entity information search engines associate with your brand.
  • Speed. Replying to a press request with "everything is at /press" takes seconds.

What belongs on the page

  1. A one-sentence description. Format it as "[Product] is a [category] that helps [audience] [outcome]." Avoid superlatives you cannot prove. This sentence will be pasted verbatim, so make it accurate and neutral.
  2. A short and a long boilerplate. Roughly 25 words and 100 words. Include the founding year, location or remote status, and what the product does today, not what the roadmap promises.
  3. Key facts. Founded date, founders and titles, headquarters or remote status, funding status if you want it public, customer count only if you can defend it, and the product's launch date. Put them in a plain list rather than an image.
  4. Founder bios and photos. Two or three sentences each, with a link to a profile page on your site and one or two professional profiles. Offer a high-resolution headshot.
  5. Brand assets. Logo in SVG and PNG, light and dark versions, a wordmark, brand colors as hex values, and usage rules such as minimum size and what not to do. Provide a single downloadable zip, plus individual files for quick grabs.
  6. Product screenshots. Three to six clean, current screenshots with no real customer data in them. Include a short caption for each, which doubles as alt text.
  7. How to name the product. Capitalization, spacing, and the correct way to refer to the company versus the product. It sounds trivial, but it prevents variants that fragment brand searches.
  8. A press contact. A real email address, ideally a role alias, and the typical response time. If you are a solo founder, say so and use your own name.

Be careful with claims, especially about AI

Journalists covering AI are skeptical, and the page will be quoted. Describe what the product does in concrete terms, and say which parts use models and which do not. If you cite a benchmark or accuracy figure, give the date, the dataset or method, and a link to the full write-up. Avoid phrases like "human-level" or "100% accurate". If you already publish a data-use or model disclosure page, link to it from here so reporters can answer the obvious privacy question without emailing you.

Add a recent mentions section

List press coverage, podcast appearances, and notable newsletter mentions with the publication name, date, headline, and a link. Keep it factual and current; remove broken links during your regular site audit. Only list coverage you actually earned, not paid placements without labeling. This section gives new writers social proof and gives you a reference when you pitch.

Publish facts that are worth citing

Reporters link to numbers and original findings more than to company descriptions. If you can share anonymized, aggregate data from your product, such as how teams use a feature or how long a typical workflow takes, put it in a short "data and research" block with the method explained. Check privacy terms first, aggregate heavily, and never expose anything that identifies a customer. Over time this becomes the page people cite.

Technical setup

  • Use a clean, stable URL such as /press and link it from the footer so it is reachable from every page.
  • Keep the page indexable and include it in your XML sitemap. Do not put the key text in an image or PDF only.
  • Add Organization structured data on your homepage or about page with your official name, logo URL, and links to your verified profiles; keep it consistent with what the press page says.
  • Host downloads on your own domain and name files clearly, for example yourproduct-logo-dark.svg, so they are easy to identify after download.
  • Date the page and keep a visible "last updated" line, then review it every quarter or after any major change.

Use the page when you pitch

A press page does not generate coverage on its own. Pair it with specific, relevant pitches: a short email that names the writer's recent article, offers one concrete angle, and links to the page. Newsletter writers and niche podcast hosts are often more responsive than national outlets, and their audiences are closer to your buyers. When coverage lands, thank the writer, add it to your mentions list, and check whether the link points to the right page. If a mention has no link, a polite note with the correct URL often fixes it.

Checklist

  1. /press is linked in the footer and listed in the sitemap.
  2. One-sentence description and two boilerplate lengths are available as selectable text.
  3. Key facts are accurate, dated, and defensible.
  4. Logos, headshots, and screenshots are downloadable on your own domain.
  5. AI claims include method, date, and a link to details.
  6. A real press contact is listed.
  7. Recent mentions are accurate and links are checked quarterly.
  8. Organization markup matches the information on the page.
