How to Collect Real Testimonials for Your AI SaaS Landing Page When You Have Almost No Customers

Founders treat testimonials as a late-stage problem — something to add "once we have real customers." That's backwards. A handful of honest quotes from your first five users almost always convert better than the generic five-star blurbs bigger competitors paste on their pages, because specific beats polished every time. Here's how to get them ethically when your customer count is still in the single digits, and how to display them so they actually do work.

1. Never fabricate — the visitor can always tell, and it's a bright line you shouldn't cross

Made-up quotes, stock-photo "customers," inflated star ratings, or a testimonial attributed to "Sarah K., CEO" with no company are common shortcuts, and they're also the fastest way to torch trust with the exact skeptical buyer you need most. Beyond the ethics, it's a bad trade practically: a visitor who suspects one quote is fake stops believing all of them, including the real ones. If you don't have real testimonials yet, an empty section beats a fabricated one — use one of the honest substitutes below instead.

2. Get quotes before you have paying customers

You don't need a customer base to get a testimonial — you need people who've actually used the product and had a real reaction. Beta testers, design partners, people from your waitlist you gave early access to, even a friend-of-a-friend founder who tried it for a real task: all of these are legitimate sources as long as the quote is real and the attribution is honest ("Beta user" or "Early access, Founder at [Company]" is fine — pretending they're a paying customer isn't).

3. Ask a question that produces a quotable answer

"Any feedback?" gets you "looks great, thanks!" — unusable. Ask instead: "What almost stopped you from trying this, and what changed your mind?" or "What's the first thing you'd tell a colleague about it?" or "What did this replace, and how long did that used to take you?" These questions pull out the specific, concrete detail that makes a testimonial believable — a number, a comparison, a moment of doubt that got resolved. Send the question by DM or email right after their first genuine win with the product, not weeks later when the moment's faded.

4. Specificity is the whole game

"This tool is amazing" persuades no one — every product claims that about itself. "I used to spend two hours writing our weekly investor update; now it's twenty minutes and I still sound like me" persuades, because it's falsifiable and concrete. When you get a vague quote back, it's fine to ask a follow-up: "Could you say roughly how much time that saved you?" Most people are happy to sharpen a quote once, as long as you're not putting words in their mouth — always send the edited version back for their approval before publishing it.

5. Attribute for real — name, role, and something verifiable

A full name, a real title, and a company name (or "independent consultant," "solo founder," etc. if that's accurate) does more trust work than any styling choice. A LinkedIn link on the name is even better — it lets a skeptical visitor verify the person exists in about four seconds, which is exactly the kind of self-serve verification that converts a doubtful reader. If someone asks to stay anonymous, respect that, but be aware an anonymous quote carries meaningfully less weight — use it, but don't lean on it alone.

6. A 20-second async video beats a written quote

You don't need a film crew. Send a Loom or phone-recorded prompt: "Would you be willing to record 20 seconds on your phone about what problem this solved for you?" A visible, unpolished face saying something specific reads as dramatically more credible than styled text, precisely because it's obviously not written by your marketing team. One real video testimonial above the fold can outperform five written quotes.

7. Where testimonials actually move the needle

  • Right after the hero. One strong quote here lowers the visitor's guard before you've asked for anything.
  • Next to the specific feature it praises. A quote about time saved belongs beside the feature that saves the time, not in a generic wall at the bottom.
  • Immediately before pricing. This is the moment of maximum hesitation — a well-placed quote here does more conversion work than another pricing tier explanation.
  • Inside the FAQ, for objection-specific quotes. A user quote that happens to address "is it hard to set up" is more convincing sitting right next to that FAQ answer than in a separate testimonials wall.

8. What to do with genuinely zero users yet

If you're pre-launch, don't force it. Honest substitutes that still build trust: a specific number from your own dogfooding ("We've drafted 40 investor updates with this internally"), a credible quote from an advisor or investor who's actually used it, or simply no social-proof section at all — replaced by an unusually clear, confident product explanation. A landing page with no testimonials but honest, specific copy converts better than one with a fabricated section, every time.

9. Refresh before quotes go stale

A testimonial mentioning a feature you've since removed, or a company that's since shut down, undermines the very credibility it was meant to add. Revisit your testimonial section every few months — swap out anything from a defunct company, and add newer, sharper quotes as you collect them. A dated but still-accurate testimonial ("Customer since 2026") is fine; a stale or now-inaccurate one is worse than none.

If you're setting up this section for the first time, start from a template with a dedicated, well-placed testimonial slot near the hero and again before pricing — so the moment you get your first honest quote, you have exactly the right place to put it.
