The AI Data-Use Disclosure Section That Builds Trust Instead of Raising Alarms

Two years ago, "we use AI" was a selling point on its own. Now it's the first thing a buyer gets nervous about. Every prospective customer has read a story about a chatbot leaking one company's data into another's answers, or a vendor quietly changing its terms to allow model training on customer content. The result: before a buyer asks about price or features, a growing number now ask "does this train on our data?" — and if your page doesn't answer that question in plain language, they'll assume the answer is yes and move on. Here's how to write a data-use section that closes that gap instead of dodging it.

1. Answer the training question in one direct sentence

Don't make a visitor infer your policy from a link to a 4,000-word privacy policy. State it outright, near the top of the section: "Your data is never used to train our models — yours or anyone else's." If the honest answer is more nuanced (you do use aggregated, anonymized data for training, or customers can opt in), say that plainly instead: "By default, your data is not used for training. You can opt in to help improve the model — off unless you turn it on." A visitor who has to go hunting for the real answer assumes you're hiding something, even when you aren't.

2. Separate "using AI" from "training on your data" — buyers conflate them

Most buyers don't distinguish between your product calling a third-party model API and your product retaining their inputs to fine-tune anything. That confusion is exactly what makes them nervous, so untangle it for them: "We send your prompts to [Model Provider] to generate a response. Neither we nor [Model Provider] store that data for training — it's processed and discarded." Naming the underlying model provider, if you use one, is a strength here, not a liability. Vague language like "powered by advanced AI" reads as evasive to a buyer who's specifically trying to find out whose model is touching their data.

3. State your retention window in days, not adjectives

"We don't keep your data longer than necessary" answers nothing. "Prompts and outputs are retained for 30 days for abuse monitoring, then permanently deleted" is a number a security reviewer can actually put in a vendor-risk spreadsheet. If you retain nothing at all beyond the request/response cycle, say that too — zero-retention is an increasingly common and genuinely differentiating claim, but only if you state it as a fact instead of implying it with soft language.

4. Put the opt-out (or opt-in) control where the buyer can find it, not just describe it

If customers can control whether their data contributes to model improvement, don't just mention the setting exists — link to exactly where it lives: "Turn this off anytime in Settings → Privacy → Model Training." A described-but-unlocatable control reads as theoretical. A buyer doing due diligence will often go check that the control is real and where you said it would be; make that check trivially easy and you've turned a skeptical visitor into a more confident one.

5. Address the multi-tenant fear directly

The specific nightmare scenario buyers have in mind — one customer's data leaking into another customer's AI output — deserves a direct, named answer, not a general privacy statement. "Your workspace's data is isolated from every other customer. Model context for your account never includes another customer's content." If your architecture supports it, a one-line technical detail (per-tenant vector stores, request-scoped context windows) does more to reassure a technical buyer than a paragraph of reassurance without specifics.

6. Don't bury this in the privacy policy — give it its own visible section

A privacy policy is where this information is legally required to live, but almost nobody reads it before signing up, and by the time they do, they've already formed a judgment. Pull the two or three sentences that actually matter — training, retention, isolation — into a short, plainly labeled section on the pricing or security page itself: "How we handle your data." Link to the full policy for the legal detail, but don't make a buyer choose between reading a wall of legal text and taking your claims on faith.

7. Match your public claims to your actual terms of service, exactly

This is the one place where a mismatch is genuinely dangerous, not just a trust problem. If your landing page says "we never train on your data" but your terms of service include a broader data-use license you haven't updated, that's a real legal exposure, not a copywriting nitpick. Before you publish a data-use section, have whoever owns your actual ToS confirm the two documents say the same thing. A buyer's legal team will read both, and a contradiction between your marketing page and your binding terms is worse for trust than never having made the claim at all.

8. Update the section the moment your model provider or policy changes

Switching model providers, adding a new AI feature, or changing your retention window all change the true answer to "does this train on our data" — and a stale data-use section is one of the fastest ways to accidentally publish a false claim. Treat this section like your pricing page: something that gets reviewed as part of any change to how the product actually works, not a paragraph written once at launch and forgotten.

The pattern underneath all of this

A data-use section builds trust for the same reason a security trust band or a comparison table does: it replaces a vague reassurance with something specific and checkable — a retention window in days, a named model provider, a settings path a buyer can actually click through to verify. The founders who lose deals here aren't the ones with an imperfect policy; they're the ones who make a buyer guess at the answer instead of stating it.

If you're building this page for a category where buyers are already primed to be careful about data — legal, HR, finance, security — start from a template with a trust and compliance section already structured, so a clear data-use disclosure fits naturally instead of feeling bolted on as an afterthought.
