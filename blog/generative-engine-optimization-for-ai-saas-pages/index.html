Generative Engine Optimization: Getting Your AI/SaaS Page Cited by ChatGPT, Perplexity, and AI Overviews

A meaningful and growing share of your next buyer's research now happens inside a chat window, not a search box. They ask ChatGPT "what's a good AI tool for X" or Perplexity "compare the top three Y platforms," and an AI assistant synthesizes an answer from a handful of sources it decided to trust — often without the buyer ever clicking through to a single website. Ranking #1 on Google for a keyword doesn't guarantee you're one of the sources an AI model chooses to cite or recommend; the two systems read pages differently and reward different things. Here's what actually improves your odds of being the tool an AI assistant names.

1. Write the one sentence a model could quote verbatim

AI answer engines tend to extract short, self-contained factual claims rather than paraphrasing a whole paragraph, so a page that never states its own value in one clean, standalone sentence gives the model nothing clean to lift. Somewhere near the top of the page, state plainly what the product is and who it's for in a sentence that would still make sense with all the surrounding context stripped away: "Scribe is an AI writing copilot that turns a rough outline into on-brand marketing copy in under a minute." Vague, scroll-triggered marketing language ("reimagining how teams create") is nearly unquotable — there's no fact in it for a model to extract.

2. Answer the comparison question before it's asked

A large share of AI-assistant queries are inherently comparative — "best AI writing tool for small teams," "X vs Y" — and the models answering them favor pages that already contain an honest comparison over pages that only pitch themselves. This is the same logic as a good "alternative to" page written for humans, but it now also functions as a direct source for a model assembling a recommendation. A page with a real, specific comparison table is far more useful to cite than one making only first-person claims about itself, because the model can lift a factual contrast instead of an unverifiable claim.

3. Make the page's facts checkable without JavaScript

Most AI crawlers fetch a page's raw HTML and either don't execute JavaScript at all or do so far less reliably than a browser does. If your pricing, feature list, or key claims only render after a client-side fetch, a model reading the page may see an empty shell where your actual content should be — the same failure mode that has always hurt traditional SEO on JS-heavy pages, just with a less forgiving crawler on the other end this time. Server-render or statically generate the facts you most want cited: price, core capability, and who it's for, so they're present in the very first response the crawler receives.

4. Publish a real llms.txt

A growing number of sites now publish a plain-text /llms.txt file at the domain root — a short, structured summary of what the site is, its key pages, and a concise description of each, written specifically for AI systems rather than human visitors. It isn't yet a universally adopted standard the way robots.txt is, and it doesn't guarantee an assistant reads it, but for the crawlers and retrieval systems that do support it, it's a cheap, direct way to hand over exactly the summary you'd want quoted instead of leaving a model to infer one from scattered page copy. Keep it factual and short — a few lines per key page, not marketing prose.

5. Get named on pages you don't control

AI assistants weight corroboration: a claim repeated across your own site, a third-party review, a comparison roundup, and a community discussion reads as more reliable than the same claim appearing only on your own marketing page, which the model has reason to treat skeptically as self-interested. This means the SEO-adjacent work of getting listed on legitimate directories, earning genuine reviews on G2 or Capterra, and being mentioned in community discussions (a relevant subreddit thread, an Indie Hackers post) does double duty now — it was always good for search and word of mouth, and it's increasingly one of the stronger signals that an AI system will trust a claim enough to repeat it.

6. Keep the FAQ literal, not clever

An FAQ section written as direct question-and-answer pairs — the same structure that earns a rich snippet in traditional search — is also one of the easiest formats for a model to lift wholesale into a chat answer, because the question-answer shape maps almost exactly onto how the assistant is being asked to respond. "Does this tool support Zapier?" followed by a plain "Yes — connect it via our native Zapier integration, no code required" is far more extractable than the same information buried in a marketing paragraph about "seamless workflow connectivity." Write FAQ answers as if they might be read aloud verbatim, because increasingly, they are.

7. Date and version your claims

Language models are trained on data with a cutoff and retrieval systems are inconsistent about how fresh a page they've indexed actually is, so an undated claim ("the fastest AI writing tool") gives a model no way to judge whether it's still true. A dated claim — "As of August 2026, generates a first draft in under 10 seconds" — gives both a human reader and a retrieval system a way to reason about currency, and it's a small habit that also protects you from an old marketing claim quietly outliving its accuracy. Update the date whenever the underlying number changes; a stale timestamp is worse than no timestamp.

8. Don't optimize for AI citation at the expense of the human reader

It's tempting to over-engineer a page into a dry list of extractable facts once you start thinking about what a model might lift from it, but the visitor who does click through from an AI answer still needs a page that reads well, builds trust, and converts — a page written purely for machine extraction reads as thin and robotic to the human who lands on it next. Treat GEO as an additional lens on the same good practices that already make a page trustworthy — clear claims, real comparisons, honest FAQs, checkable facts — not a separate, competing set of rules that pulls the copy in a worse direction for people.

The pattern underneath all of this

Getting cited by an AI assistant rewards almost exactly the same qualities that have always made a page trustworthy to a skeptical human reader: specific claims over vague ones, checkable facts over adjectives, and corroboration from sources you don't control. The main new work is technical, not rhetorical — make sure the facts you want repeated are actually present in the raw HTML a crawler sees, and give retrieval systems a clean, literal summary to work from instead of forcing them to guess at one.

If you're building a content-heavy AI/SaaS page where this matters most, start from a template that already renders its real copy, pricing, and FAQ as plain, crawlable HTML rather than behind client-side rendering — the same choice that's always been good for traditional SEO turns out to be exactly what an AI crawler needs too.
