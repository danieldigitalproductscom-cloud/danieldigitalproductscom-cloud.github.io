The About Page That Builds Trust When You're a Team of One (or Two)

AI tooling has made it possible for one person to design a logo, write the copy, build the product, and launch a landing page in a weekend — which is exactly why buyers have gotten more suspicious of small teams, not less. A visitor who's been burned by an abandoned side-project SaaS or a vague "our team of experts" page that turned out to be one person and a template now actively looks for the tell. The instinct is to hide the small headcount behind vague plural language. That backfires. Here's how to build an About page that makes a small team a reason to trust you instead of a reason to hesitate.

1. State the real number, plainly

"Our team is dedicated to..." reads as evasive the moment a buyer notices there's no team photo, no names, no headcount anywhere on the site — it implies more people than exist without technically lying, and skeptical readers have learned to spot the pattern. Say it outright instead: "Threadly is built and maintained by two people." A precise, confident number reads as more trustworthy than a vague plural, because it's a claim a buyer can't catch you inflating later.

2. Use a real photo, not a stock avatar or an AI-generated headshot

A generic illustrated avatar or an obviously AI-generated headshot next to a founder's name is one of the fastest ways to undo the trust a plain-spoken bio just built — buyers who've seen enough of these now recognize the pattern instantly and wonder what else on the page isn't quite real. A real photo, even an unpolished one taken on a phone, does more work than a professionally lit stock image that looks like it belongs to someone else's team page.

3. Link to something a skeptic could actually verify

"10+ years of experience in AI and SaaS" is a claim; a LinkedIn profile, a GitHub with real commit history, or a blog with posts dated years back is evidence for that claim. Link to at least one place a buyer could go check that you are who you say you are and have built the things you say you've built. An unlinked bio asks for faith; a linked one survives due diligence.

4. Answer the bus-factor question before it's asked

Buyers evaluating a small vendor are quietly running the same risk calculation a security reviewer runs on a bigger one: what happens to my data and my workflow if this team disappears? Address it directly instead of hoping nobody asks: "Your data exports to CSV/JSON anytime from Settings — no lock-in, no support ticket required." A concrete, self-serve answer to continuity risk removes the single biggest objection a cautious buyer has to trusting a small team with something they depend on daily.

5. Frame small-team speed as the advantage, not an apology

Founders often write around their headcount as if it's a weakness to minimize. It's frequently the opposite of what a frustrated buyer of bloated enterprise software wants: "We ship fixes within days, not through a quarterly release cycle — there's no committee to route a bug fix through." A small team's actual advantage is real responsiveness; say so plainly instead of burying the headcount and hoping nobody does the math on how a "team" replies to support emails within the hour.

6. Promise a support response time you can personally keep

"24/7 enterprise support" from a two-person team is a promise you can't keep, and the first support ticket that goes unanswered for two days quietly confirms every doubt the vague About page tried to paper over. A smaller, honest commitment — "I personally read and reply to every support email within one business day" — is both more credible and, unlike the enterprise version, a promise you can actually hold yourself to every single time.

7. Be upfront about where you use AI in your own operations

If AI drafts your support replies, writes your changelog summaries, or triages incoming tickets before a human reads them, say so in a short, matter-of-fact line rather than letting a buyer discover it and wonder what else was quietly automated without disclosure. "AI drafts a first-pass reply to routine questions; a human reviews and sends every response" is honest and, for most buyers, entirely reasonable — the trust problem isn't using AI internally, it's a mismatch between what a buyer assumes and what's actually happening behind the page.

8. Never invent a team you don't have

Fabricated employee names, stock headshots labeled with fake titles, or LinkedIn-style bios for people who don't exist are a real and increasingly common pattern buyers now watch for specifically, and it's not just a trust risk — it's the kind of deceptive claim that erodes the entire relationship the moment it's discovered, no matter how good the product is underneath it. If it's really just you, the honest version of that story is more compelling than a manufactured one, not less.

The pattern underneath all of this

An About page builds trust the same way a comparison table or a security section does: by replacing a vague, unverifiable impression with something specific and checkable — a real number, a linked profile, a promise sized to what you can actually deliver. Buyers aren't looking for a big team. They're looking for a team, however small, that's telling them the truth about what it is.

If you're building this page for a product where buyers are already trusting you with something personal — their inbox, their calendar, their daily workflow — pair an honest About page with a landing page that earns the same trust throughout, not just in one section reserved for biographies.
