Subdomain vs. Subdirectory: Where to Put Your AI SaaS Blog for SEO

Most founders pick a blog URL structure in about thirty seconds, usually because it's whatever their CMS defaulted to, and never think about it again. That default is rarely neutral. Whether your content lives at example.com/blog or blog.example.com changes whether every article you write compounds into your main domain's authority — or builds up a separate, weaker entity that Google evaluates on its own. Get it right before you've published fifty posts, because moving them later costs you real traffic.

1. The short answer: use a subdirectory unless something forces your hand

Put content at example.com/blog, not blog.example.com, whenever you have a real choice. A subdirectory is unambiguously part of the same site in every ranking system Google uses — the backlinks your blog earns, the topical relevance it builds, and the trust it accumulates all flow directly into the domain your product pages live on. A subdomain is treated far more cautiously: historically, and still today in many cases, Google's systems can evaluate it as a related-but-distinct property, which means your best-performing blog post might build authority that never meaningfully helps example.com/pricing rank for anything.

2. Why founders end up on a subdomain anyway

It's almost never a deliberate SEO decision — it's a tooling one. Marketing sites built on a page builder (Webflow, Framer, Carrd) or a hosted CMS (Ghost, Ghost(Pro), a separate WordPress instance) often can't be mounted at a path on the same domain as a statically-hosted or differently-hosted marketing site, so blog.example.com becomes the path of least resistance. That's a legitimate constraint, but it's worth knowing you're trading engineering convenience for search compounding before you commit a domain's worth of content to it.

3. How to get a subdirectory even when your blog runs on different software

You don't need your blog CMS and your marketing site to be the same codebase — you need them to answer requests under the same hostname. A reverse proxy or edge rule does this cleanly: point example.com/blog/* at your Ghost, Webflow, or headless CMS instance while the root domain keeps serving your static landing page. Cloudflare Workers, Netlify's _redirects/reverse-proxy rules, Vercel rewrites, and most CDNs support this directly, usually as a single rule. This is the single highest-leverage technical SEO decision most pre-revenue AI SaaS teams never make, because it looks like a devops task instead of a marketing one.

4. If you're already on a subdomain, don't panic-migrate

Moving an established blog.example.com to example.com/blog is the right long-term move if you have real content volume and search traffic worth protecting — but it's a real migration, not a config change, and it will cost you a temporary traffic dip even done perfectly. Before you touch anything: export a full list of every indexed blog URL from Search Console, plan a 1:1 301 redirect from every old subdomain URL to its new subdirectory equivalent, and never redirect everything to the new blog's homepage — that flattens your accumulated per-article rankings instead of transferring them. Expect a two-to-eight week period where rankings wobble while Google re-crawls and re-associates the URLs; that's normal, not a sign something broke.

5. What doesn't need to move: docs, help centers, and status pages

The subdirectory rule mostly applies to content you want to rank for its own keywords and drive organic discovery — your blog, your comparison pages, your glossary. Documentation and help centers are a closer call: they benefit less from search-authority compounding (people mostly reach docs through in-product links or branded search, not a random Google query) and more often live on infrastructure genuinely separate from your marketing site. It's still worth putting docs under /docs if you can do it cheaply, but it's a lower-priority fight than getting your blog right.

6. Pick your permanent structure before your first ten posts, not your first hundred

The cost of this decision is asymmetric: setting it up correctly on day one costs an afternoon of reverse-proxy configuration. Fixing it after a year of content means a full redirect migration, a temporary ranking dip, and re-doing internal links across every post that pointed to the old URLs. If you're choosing your blog's home today, that afternoon is the cheapest SEO investment you'll make all quarter.

7. One more decision while you're here: trailing slashes and www

The same "pick one and never change it" logic applies to two smaller decisions that get skipped for the same reason: whether URLs end in a trailing slash, and whether your canonical domain is www.example.com or bare example.com. Neither choice is inherently better for rankings, but inconsistency — the same page reachable at four different URL variants with no canonical tag pointing to one — splits the authority a single page would otherwise accumulate. Set a 301 redirect from every variant to your chosen canonical form, and add a <link rel="canonical"> tag as a backstop even after the redirects are in place.

The pattern underneath all of this

Every URL structure decision on a marketing site answers the same underlying question: does this page's authority accumulate into the one domain you're trying to build, or does it leak into a separate entity Google evaluates on its own? Subdirectories, consistent canonical URLs, and 1:1 redirects during any migration all exist to keep that answer "it accumulates" — every time, for every page you ever publish.

If you're setting up your content site now, starting from a template already structured with clean, consistent URL and metadata patterns means this is a one-time configuration decision — not a redirect migration you're doing a year from now with an audience already watching.
