Landing Pages for AI Browser Extensions: What to Show Before Someone Clicks "Add to Chrome"

A growing share of AI products don't live behind a signup form — they live in the browser toolbar, reading a page, drafting a reply, or overlaying a suggestion on top of whatever the user is already doing. That's a genuinely different purchase decision than "start a free trial." An extension asks for permission to sit inside every page someone visits, and most people have installed at least one extension that slowed their browser to a crawl or quietly sold their browsing data. Your landing page is doing double duty: selling the product, and un-doing that skepticism before the visitor even reaches the store listing. Here's what that page needs that a normal SaaS page doesn't.

1. Show the "before," not just the "after"

Most SaaS pages jump straight to the polished output. Extension pages convert better when they first show the ordinary screen the visitor already recognizes — a Gmail inbox, a LinkedIn profile, a checkout page — and then show your product's overlay appearing on top of it. That single before/after frame does more explaining than three paragraphs of feature copy, because it answers the actual question a visitor has: "what does this look like sitting inside the app I already use all day?"

2. Put the install button where the click actually happens

Don't send visitors to a generic "get started" flow — link the primary button directly to the Chrome Web Store (or Edge Add-ons, Firefox Add-ons) listing, and say so on the button itself: "Add to Chrome — it's free." A visitor who clicks and lands somewhere unexpected loses trust immediately. If you support multiple browsers, detect the visitor's browser and default to the matching store link rather than making them pick from a list.

3. Address permissions before the browser's permission dialog does

Every extension install ends with a browser-native dialog listing what the extension can access — "Read and change all your data on the websites you visit" reads as alarming out of context, even for a legitimate tool. Get ahead of it with a short, plain-language section: what data the extension reads, why it needs to, and what it explicitly does not do with it ("processed in your browser, never stored on our servers" if that's true). A visitor who's already been reassured won't be spooked by the dialog; one who hasn't will often cancel the install right there.

4. Screenshot the actual UI surface, not a mockup browser chrome

Extensions typically have two or three UI surfaces — a toolbar popup, an in-page overlay, and sometimes an options page — and each looks different from a typical SaaS dashboard screenshot. Show all of them, captioned by what they do, rather than one hero image of a generic laptop with a browser window on it. Visitors evaluating an extension are specifically trying to picture where the product will physically appear on their screen; a vague dashboard shot doesn't answer that.

5. Borrow the store's own social proof, verbatim

If you have a rating and review count on the Chrome Web Store, display it on the landing page with a link back to the listing — "4.8 stars, 2,300+ reviews" carries more weight than a handful of hand-picked testimonial quotes, because visitors know it's independently verifiable and can go check it themselves in one click. Early on, before you have volume, a raw, real review count ("47 reviews") is still more credible than none at all; don't manufacture a bigger number.

6. Make "no lock-in" explicit

Uninstalling a SaaS account usually means a support ticket or a buried settings page; uninstalling an extension is one right-click away, and visitors know it. Use that as a selling point instead of hiding from it: "Uninstall anytime in two clicks, no email required" lowers the perceived cost of trying the product to nearly zero, which matters more for an impulse-install decision than it does for a considered SaaS signup.

7. Cover the free-vs-paid split before the store listing does

Store listings show "Free" in bold next to your extension's name regardless of whether some features sit behind a paid tier — so if you monetize with upgrades, say exactly where the free line sits on your landing page before the visitor installs and discovers it themselves. "Free forever for up to 50 replies/month, upgrade for unlimited" avoids the review-tanking surprise of someone installing expecting a fully free tool and feeling misled a week later.

8. Write for the exact query, not just the brand

People search "AI extension for Gmail" or "browser extension to summarize articles" far more than they search a specific product name pre-launch. Make sure your title tag, H1, and at least one section heading contain the literal task and the literal surface ("for Gmail," "for Chrome," "for LinkedIn") rather than only your tagline — this is one of the highest-intent, lowest-competition search patterns in AI tooling right now, and most extension landing pages still optimize only for their own brand name.

The mistakes that quietly kill extension installs

  • Generic dashboard screenshots. If nothing on the page shows the extension actually sitting inside a browser, visitors can't picture using it.
  • Silence on permissions. The browser will show the scary dialog regardless — better it isn't the first time the visitor hears about data access.
  • A "start free trial" button that isn't the store link. Extra steps between intent and install lose people who would have installed on the spot.
  • Hiding the paid tier until after install. It shows up as a one-star review calling the product a bait-and-switch, which then sits on your store listing forever.

The underlying shift is small but real: an extension landing page sells a moment of trust inside someone else's browser, not just a product. Show exactly where it appears, exactly what it touches, and exactly how to leave, and the install becomes an easy yes instead of a risk to think over.
