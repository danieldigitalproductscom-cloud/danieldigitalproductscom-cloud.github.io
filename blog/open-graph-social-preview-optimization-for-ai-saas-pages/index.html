Open Graph & Social Preview Optimization for AI SaaS Landing Pages

Founders spend weeks polishing a landing page, then launch it with a tweet or LinkedIn post that renders as a bare blue link, or worse, a random screenshot with a cropped logo and no headline. The page itself was never the problem — the missing or wrong Open Graph tags were. A link preview is the first impression for nearly everyone who finds you through social sharing, and it's controlled by a handful of meta tags most templates get half-right. Here's how to get all of it right.

1. The four tags that actually control the preview

Every page that might get shared needs, at minimum: og:title, og:description, og:image, and og:url. Don't assume the crawler will fall back to your <title> and meta description sensibly — some platforms do, some show nothing, and none of them do it consistently enough to rely on. Write og:title and og:description as their own fields, even if they start as a copy of your existing tags. This also lets you write a punchier, more "share-worthy" version of your headline without touching your actual SEO title, which has different constraints.

2. Build a real og:image — don't let a crawler screenshot your page

Without an explicit og:image, some platforms fall back to grabbing the first image on the page, which is often a logo, an icon, or nothing at all. Design a dedicated share image: 1200×630px, the standard size that renders correctly across X, LinkedIn, Facebook, and Slack without awkward cropping. Put your product name, a short benefit-driven headline, and your accent color as the background — treat it as a tiny billboard, not a screenshot. Keep all text inside the center 1200×600px safe zone; some clients crop the outer edges on certain aspect ratios, and text near the border gets clipped.

3. Don't reuse one og:image sitewide

A single generic share image for every page — homepage, pricing, blog posts — means every link you share looks identical in a feed, and readers scrolling past lose the cue that tells them what's different about this particular link. Generate a distinct image per page type at minimum: one for the homepage, one template for blog posts that pulls in the article title, one for pricing. If you're on a static site with no build step, even three or four hand-made variants swapped in per page are a large improvement over one.

4. Twitter/X needs its own card tags — don't assume it reads Open Graph

X's crawler prioritizes its own twitter:card, twitter:title, twitter:description, and twitter:image tags over Open Graph equivalents, and will render a broken or missing preview if only OG tags are present. Set twitter:card to summary_large_image so the image renders full-width instead of as a small square thumbnail — the large format gets meaningfully more clicks in a feed than the summary-card default. You can point twitter:image at the same file as og:image; you don't need a second design, just the second set of tags.

5. Absolute URLs only, every time

A relative path like /og-image.png works fine in a browser but fails silently for most social crawlers, which need a full https://yourdomain.com/og-image.png. This is the single most common reason a preview shows no image at all despite the tag being present in the page source — the tag is technically there, but the crawler can't resolve where the file actually lives. Double-check og:url the same way: it should be the canonical absolute URL of the page, not a tracking-parameter-laden link someone happened to share.

6. Cache-bust after you change the image

Social platforms cache a link's preview aggressively — sometimes for weeks — keyed to the URL, not the image content. If you update your share image and the old one keeps showing up, the fix isn't waiting; it's forcing a re-scrape. LinkedIn's Post Inspector and X's Card Validator both offer a manual "re-scrape this URL" action, and running your URL through them after any og:image change is worth the thirty seconds it takes, especially right before a launch when you want the new design live immediately, not whenever the cache happens to expire.

7. Test before you post, not after

Paste your URL into LinkedIn's Post Inspector and X's Card Validator before you actually publish the launch post. Both tools show you exactly what a reader will see, and both catch the two failure modes that matter most: a missing image (usually a relative-path or file-not-found issue) and awkward cropping (usually a sizing or safe-zone issue). Five minutes of testing here prevents the worst outcome — a broken preview on the one post that was supposed to drive your launch-day traffic.

8. Keep descriptions scannable, not keyword-stuffed

The og:description shows up small, often truncated after 100–150 characters depending on the platform. Front-load the value: "Turn a prompt into a polished slide deck in minutes" beats "AI-powered presentation generation platform for sales, marketing, and startup teams" — the first is legible at a glance in a crowded feed, the second gets cut off before it says anything concrete. Write it for a human scrolling past, not for a search engine; this tag has no SEO ranking weight, its only job is to earn the click.

The takeaway

Open Graph tags are a small, one-time setup that pays off every single time someone shares your link from then on — in a launch thread, a newsletter roundup, or a Slack message from a happy customer. Get the four core tags right, design a real share image instead of hoping a crawler picks something reasonable, add the Twitter-specific tags separately, and test before every major post. It's a couple hours of work that determines whether your best marketing moments look credible or broken.

If design isn't your strength, start from a template with an established color system already defined — pulling your accent colors and product name into a share image is a five-minute job once the palette is already decided, rather than a design project of its own.
