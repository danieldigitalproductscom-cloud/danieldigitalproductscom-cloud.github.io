A Realistic Backlink Strategy for a Pre-Revenue AI/SaaS Startup

Search engines still treat backlinks as one of the strongest trust signals a domain can earn, and a brand-new AI/SaaS site has none. The advice most founders find first — "hire a PR agency" or "get featured in TechCrunch" — assumes resources a pre-revenue startup doesn't have. The tactics below don't. They're slower and less glamorous, but they're what actually moves a zero-authority domain forward in the first few months, and they compound instead of expiring the day a campaign ends.

1. Publish one real number nobody else has

Journalists, newsletter writers, and other founders link to data, not opinions. You don't need a formal study — you need one specific, citable number pulled from your own usage, a small survey, or a public dataset you've analyzed in a way nobody else has. "We looked at 200 public AI/SaaS pricing pages and found 61% hide their price behind 'Contact us'" is link bait; "AI is changing how SaaS gets sold" is not. Publish the number as its own page with a clear methodology section — that's the page people actually cite, because it gives them something concrete to point at.

2. Answer journalist requests where you're a genuine source

Services like Connectively (formerly HARO), Qwoted, and the #journorequests hashtag on X surface reporters looking for quotes on specific topics. Filter aggressively — only respond when you're a legitimate expert on the exact question asked, not "AI" in general. A specific, well-written 100-word answer to "how are startups pricing AI features" from someone who actually builds AI/SaaS pricing pages gets used; a generic pitch about your product does not. One quote in a mid-tier trade publication is worth more than ten unused pitches.

3. Build something free that solves one narrow problem

A free, no-signup tool — a pricing-page grader, a landing-page speed checker, a headline analyzer for your specific niche — earns links the way blog posts rarely do, because other people write about tools, not opinions. It doesn't need to be sophisticated; it needs to solve one problem completely in under thirty seconds. Submit it to Product Hunt, relevant subreddits, and niche Slack/Discord communities where your exact audience already gathers, and let the tool itself do the earning instead of asking for a link directly.

4. Write the comparison page your competitors won't

"X vs Y" and "alternatives to X" pages get organically linked from Reddit threads, forum posts, and other founders' own comparison content, because people constantly search for and reference exactly that framing. Write it honestly — include categories where a competitor genuinely wins — and it becomes something people are comfortable linking to as a neutral reference, rather than obvious self-promotion nobody wants to be seen sharing.

5. Trade real estate with adjacent (not competing) tools

An AI meeting-notes tool and an AI project-management tool share an audience without competing for the same purchase. A short "integrations" or "works well with" page linking out to five complementary tools, paired with a polite email asking if they'd add a reciprocal mention, converts more often than it should — most small SaaS teams are flattered to be asked and will trade a link for a link. This only works with genuinely adjacent products; links traded with direct competitors or irrelevant sites read as manipulative to both users and search engines.

6. Find broken links pointing at your competitors' dead pages

Tools like Ahrefs' free backlink checker or a simple site: search plus a link-checker extension will surface pages that used to link to a competitor's now-404'd resource. Email the site owner, point out the dead link, and offer your equivalent page as the replacement. This is unglamorous, manual work, but the success rate is meaningfully higher than a cold pitch, because you're solving the site owner's problem (a broken link) rather than only asking for something.

7. Be selective about directories — most are worthless now

Mass directory submission was a viable strategy a decade ago; today, most general web directories carry no authority and some carry risk if they're flagged as link farms. Submit only to directories your actual buyers browse: AlternativeTo, a well-maintained "awesome AI tools" GitHub list relevant to your category, G2 and Capterra (which double as review sources), and category-specific curated lists with real editorial standards. A link from a directory nobody visits does nothing for rankings and nothing for referral traffic — skip it.

8. Let your own content get cited by writing the definitive version

When another founder or blogger needs to link to an explanation of a concept in your space, they link to whichever page explains it best — not whichever page is trying hardest to sell something. A genuinely thorough, well-structured explainer on a term or process central to your niche (not your product) becomes a natural citation target over time, the same way Stripe's guides get linked from countless unrelated fintech articles. This is the slowest tactic on this list and the one with the highest ceiling.

What to skip entirely

  • Paid link packages. "50 backlinks for $99" services sell links from low-quality, often penalized networks. They can actively hurt rankings, and the pattern is easy for search engines to detect.
  • Guest post farms. Sites that will publish anything for a fee, with no editorial review, carry the same risk as paid links — the link is worth roughly nothing and the association can be worse.
  • Reciprocal-link schemes at scale. One or two genuine partner swaps is normal business; joining a "link exchange" network of dozens of unrelated sites is a pattern search engines are specifically built to discount.

The pattern underneath all of this

Every tactic that actually works here has the same shape: create something worth linking to — a number, a tool, an honest comparison, a definitive explainer — and then make it easy for the right small set of people to find it. Nothing above requires a budget, a press contact, or an existing audience. It requires picking one tactic, doing it properly for a real target list instead of a mass blast, and treating the first few links as the slow start of a compounding asset rather than a one-time campaign to finish and forget.
